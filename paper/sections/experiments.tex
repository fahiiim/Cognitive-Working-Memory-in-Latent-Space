\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

\paragraph{Protocol.} All rules read the same frozen backbone checkpoints, except the jointly trained
methods, which train their own. Thresholds are selected on ID-val (\cref{sec:method}), and each OOD
split is evaluated exactly once. For a test set $\{(x_n,y_n)\}_{n=1}^N$ and a rule that halts input $x_n$
at step $\tau_n$, we report exact-match accuracy over all output positions, the mean number of steps,
and two error modes measured against the per-instance bound of \cref{eq:lb}:
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{PS} = \frac1N\sum_{n=1}^N \ind\bigl[\tau_n < \LB(x_n)\bigr],
  \qquad
  \mathrm{PE} = \frac1N\sum_{n=1}^N \ind\bigl[\tau_n < \LB(x_n)\bigr]\,
  \ind\bigl[\yhat^{(\tau_n)}(x_n)\ne y_n\bigr].
  \label{eq:premature}
\end{equation}
The \emph{premature-stop rate} PS counts uncertified answers, and the \emph{premature-error rate}
$\mathrm{PE}\le\mathrm{PS}$ counts those that are also wrong. The brackets in the accuracy tables are
percentile-bootstrap intervals over the two seeds, so they span roughly the seed range; premature rates
use a bootstrap over test instances pooled across seeds. With two seeds a Wilcoxon signed-rank test
cannot reach significance (the smallest attainable $p$ is $0.5$), so the pre-registered Holm-corrected
tests are reported in \texttt{results/paper\_numbers.json} but no claim rests on them; the effects we
discuss are tens of points, far above seed-to-seed variation. The objective pilot was a tie, so both
backbones use the fixed prior (\texttt{PREREGISTRATION.md}).

\paragraph{Backbones (Gate G1).} We first check that the backbones can solve the deep splits at all, by
stopping them with the stable oracle. The addition backbone is then correct on 99.6\% of OOD-both
instances, whose depths reach $12.8\times$ the training maximum (\cref{fig:oracle}), so it passes G1. The
parity backbone reaches 100\% on OOD-depth and OOD-length but only 85\% on OOD-both, and its per-step
accuracy decays after the peak (\cref{fig:stepacc}). It fails G1, and on parity we therefore measure how
stopping rules behave on an imperfect backbone. Adaptive halting is clearly needed: a fixed step count
chosen on ID-val is correct on only 71\% (addition) and 31\% (parity) of OOD-depth instances.

\paragraph{Pooled gates leak length (H1).} The jointly trained pooled gates look excellent in
distribution, and even on OOD-depth, but collapse as soon as the inputs grow longer
(\cref{tab:main_addition}). On addition, ACT with the tuned ponder weight $\tauact=0.01$ is correct on
99.8\% of OOD-depth instances in only 2.4 steps, far below the light-cone bound, yet on just 43.2\% of
OOD-length and 35.9\% of OOD-both instances. Inspecting its decisions reveals a shortcut. ACT halts at
step~1 when no carry can enter the long run of nines and at step~5 otherwise, and the backbone's default
output for a pending position switches from ``no carry'' to ``carry'' at around step~4. A pooled gate can
read this global bit reliably when the input contains a single long run, but the bit becomes useless
once there are many runs. PonderNet, the v1 gate and both TRM-style pooled correctness heads fail in the
same way, reaching 47.6\%, 69.7\%, 50.0\% (post hoc) and 31.9\% (joint) on OOD-both. Their step counts are
nearly flat in depth (\cref{fig:steps}): out of distribution they spend what they spent in training.
Parity is more forgiving in depth, where ACT and PonderNet do scale their steps and reach 98.9\% and
96.7\% on OOD-depth, but both fall below 1\% on OOD-both. The v1 gate did not train on parity at all
(3\% ID accuracy): it never halts, and its halting-weighted loss degrades the backbone.

\paragraph{Premature stopping is not premature error (H2).} The convergence rules, $k$-step stability
and the KL between steps, halt before $\LB(x)$ on 75\% of OOD-depth and 50\% of OOD-both addition
instances (\cref{tab:premature}), and yet they remain 99.8\% accurate. Pooled over the deep splits,
their premature-error rate is 0.1\%, compared with 18--23\% for ACT, PonderNet and the joint pooled head.
This is precisely the gap that \cref{thm:lb} leaves open: the answers are uncertified, but the test data
do not contain the flip partner. The backbone's default guess for a pending digit is usually right, and
while a carry wave is still travelling the hidden state keeps changing, so convergence is a good proxy
for completion. Parity shows the effect in its sharpest form: one step before the bound a position has
seen every bit of its segment, only the closing delimiter lies outside its window, and the natural
guess that no further bits follow is correct on the actual input. This is why the stable oracle and ACT
can be accurate on parity OOD-depth while halting before the bound. The hidden-state-change rule, whose threshold is fitted on short inputs,
halts after about four steps on OOD-both and is correct on 42.7\% of instances. On parity, where outputs
keep flipping as information arrives, confidence is actively misleading: max-softmax halts before the
bound on 91\% of OOD-depth instances and is correct on only 51\%, and on OOD-both on 0.8\%. Only the KL
rule partly survives, with 94\% on OOD-depth and 57\% on OOD-both. Since both H1 and H2 hold, Gate G2
passes.

\paragraph{Per-position certificates on addition (H3).} On addition, every per-position rule halts at
the light-cone bound on every split, with no premature stops and at least 99\% accuracy. Max-softmax,
entropy and all four completion-head variants use 13.5 steps on OOD-depth and 75.4--75.6 steps on
OOD-both, against 13.5 and 75.4 for the oracle $\LB(x)$. The theory explains why. A pending addition
digit has two equally plausible outputs, carry or no carry, so a calibrated decoder is uncertain at
exactly the positions that are not yet determined, and $S_{\mathrm{msp}}$ in \cref{eq:posthoc}, a
minimum over positions, becomes a local certificate in the sense of \cref{cor:local}. A linear probe
recovers the pending bit from the hidden state with AUC above 0.999 on every split (\cref{tab:probes}).
Per-position confidence on addition therefore already implements the automaton of
\cref{prop:automaton}, and a learned completion head has nothing to add. Gate G3, which required a
10-point gain over the best baseline, fails on addition by construction.

\paragraph{Completion heads on parity learn the clock.} On parity, all completion heads halt after
about nine steps regardless of depth, close to the largest training depth. They are correct on 31--34\%
of OOD-depth and 0\% of OOD-both instances, well below ACT and PonderNet on OOD-depth, and the joint and
propagating variants behave no differently. The cause lies in the training data. During training every
position is done by step~10, so the targets in \cref{eq:done} make ``late'' and ``done''
indistinguishable, and the parity backbone's hidden state encodes elapsed time well enough for the head
to exploit it. The propagating head of \cref{eq:prop} was designed to wait for its neighbours, but
nothing in the data forces it to, since a neighbour is also always done by step~10. This is the failure
of the pooled gates in another guise, with the shortcut moved from the input length to the step count.
G3 fails on parity as well, and following the pre-registered pivot we read the results as a map of when
simple signals suffice rather than as a win for a new halting method.

\paragraph{Decoupling (H4) and compute.} Training a completion head jointly changes the backbone's
step-averaged ID-val accuracy by less than 0.02 points on both tasks, so the auxiliary target leaves the
task solution undisturbed. Gates that reweight the task loss do disturb it. Relative to the base model,
ACT lowers this accuracy by 34 points and the v1 gate by 17 points on addition (86 points for the v1 gate
on parity), because steps after the learned halting point receive little task gradient. In FLOPs, a
completion head costs one pointwise MLP per position and step, and the cost of every rule is dominated
by the number of steps it takes (\cref{fig:compute}). Wall-clock savings are smaller at our scale. With
online early exit on 512 OOD-depth instances, max-softmax takes 19.8\,ms against 23.8\,ms for the full
budget, while the completion heads take 23.8 and 28.3\,ms (\cref{tab:probes}), because the host
synchronisation and re-batching after every step cost about as much as the steps saved. Converting
certified halting into latency will need fused halting checks or larger models.

\paragraph{Ablations.} Removing input re-injection leaves OOD-depth intact but destroys OOD-both (stable
oracle 0.6\%). Halving the width to 64 lowers the stable oracle only to 95.2\% but max-softmax to 48.0\%:
with less capacity, the decoder's confidence stops tracking the pending state. Two convolutions per step
($r=2$) reach 100\% on both (\cref{tab:probes}); each ablation uses a single seed. Trade-off curves
and premature stopping by depth are in \cref{fig:tradeoff,fig:premature_depth}.
