\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Recurrent and looped models solve harder problems by thinking longer: the same block is applied again
and again, and more iterations buy more reasoning
\citep{schwarzschild2021thinking,bansal2022endtoend,geiping2025scaling,jolicoeur2025trm}. The promise is
adaptive compute, spending few steps on easy inputs and many on hard ones. Keeping it requires a
stopping rule, and the rules in use (learned halting gates \citep{graves2016act,banino2021pondernet},
confidence thresholds, and convergence tests \citep{zhou2020pabee,geiping2025scaling}) are fitted on the
difficulty seen in training. The interesting inputs are the ones that are harder than anything seen in
training, and there a stopping rule has to decide, from information the model has never been rewarded
for reading, that the computation is finished.

We study this decision in a setting where the right answer to ``when is it finished?'' is known. For a
local recurrent reasoner, where each step moves information a fixed number of positions, a light-cone
argument gives every input a lower bound $\mathrm{LB}(x)$ on the step at which its answer can be
certified (\cref{thm:lb}). Halting earlier returns an answer that the model's own information does not
determine, which is correct only by luck of the data. The bound depends on the input's \emph{depth}, not
its length, so it separates two kinds of distribution shift that are usually confounded: longer inputs
and deeper ones. A local, size-invariant automaton can halt exactly at the bound for every length
(\cref{prop:automaton}), so the bound is achievable, and we can ask which learned or post-hoc rules
achieve it.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Theory.} A light-cone lower bound for local recurrent reasoners that turns ``halting too
  early'' into a per-instance, checkable quantity, with corollaries for local commit-on-done rules and
  for rules that see only the input length, and closed-form depth oracles plus a matching automaton for
  addition and segmented parity (\cref{sec:theory}). Every statement has an executable test.
  \item \textbf{Benchmark.} DepthShift: tasks with exact per-instance depth, and frozen splits that vary
  depth and length independently, up to $12.8\times$ the training depth (\cref{sec:benchmark}).
  \item \textbf{Evaluation.} Fifteen stopping rules and four references on one backbone family (post-hoc
  confidence and convergence rules, ACT, PonderNet, TRM-style and v1 pooled heads, and local completion
  heads that we introduce), scored by accuracy, steps, and premature stops and errors relative to
  $\mathrm{LB}(x)$ (\cref{sec:method,sec:experiments}).
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Findings.} (i) Jointly trained pooled gates leak length: on addition, ACT is 99.8\% accurate on
deeper inputs while using fewer steps than the bound allows, by reading a global shortcut, and falls to
35.9\% once inputs are both deeper and longer. (ii) Premature stopping and premature error are different
things: global convergence rules halt before the bound on most deep addition inputs and are still
right, because a travelling carry keeps the state moving. (iii) When pending positions are genuinely
ambiguous, as in addition, per-position confidence is already a certificate: max-softmax halts at
exactly $\mathrm{LB}(x)$ on every split, and our completion heads match it without improving on it.
(iv) When they are not, as in parity, the completion heads, although designed after the automaton,
learn the training clock and halt at the training depth, and pooled gates extrapolate in depth but
fall below 1\% once inputs are also longer. (v) Jointly trained completion heads leave the backbone's
solution unchanged, whereas gates that reweight the task loss degrade it. Taken together, the results
say that the difficulty of termination detection lies less in the readout architecture than in
training data that never shows a computation unfinished at a late step.
